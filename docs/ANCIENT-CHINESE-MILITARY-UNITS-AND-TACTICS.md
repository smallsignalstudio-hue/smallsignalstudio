# ANCIENT / IMPERIAL CHINESE MILITARY UNITS AND TACTICS — Research Brief (Tower-Defence Game Doc)

> English research notes for a tower-defence game bible. Terms given in **Chinese (characters + Hanyu Pinyin)**.  
> **Method:** Prefer primary evidence (*Shiji* 史記, *Hanshu* 漢書, *Sunzi* 孫子 / *Sun Bin* 孫臏 bamboo texts, *Mozi* 墨子 siege chapters, Qin terracotta archaeology, Han frontier slips); flag Romance / popular myth vs history explicitly. Do **not** treat Qing Banner armies, Dynasty Warriors kit, or uncritical Osprey reconstructions as primary.

### Core primary sources
| Source | Nature / caution |
|---|---|
| **Oracle bones** (Shang 商) | Campaign verbs, enemy ethnonyms, chariot/horse vocabulary—ritual-administrative, not TO&E. |
| **Bronze inscriptions** (Western Zhou 西周) | Royal appointments, chariot gifts, war booty; elite ideology. |
| ***Zuo Zhuan* 左傳** | Spring–Autumn campaign narratives; richest pre-imperial narrative military text—compiled/edited later; numbers rhetorical. |
| ***Sunzi Bingfa* 孫子兵法** | Warring States military theory (traditional attribution to Sun Wu of Wu); Yinqueshan bamboo confirms early Han circulation of 13-chapter core + extra fragments. **Not** a field manual with unit sizes. |
| ***Sun Bin Bingfa* 孫臏兵法** | Recovered Yinqueshan 1972; reflects mid–late Warring States (crossbow, siege, cavalry emphasis). Distinct from *Sunzi*. |
| ***Mozi* 墨子** ch. 52–71 (defence corpus) | Technical city-defence checklists; many chapters lost/corrupt; ideal “best-case” fortress, not every city. Johnston complete translation recommended. |
| ***Weiliaozi* 尉繚子**, ***Liutao* 六韜**, ***Wuzi* 吳子** | Other Warring States / early Han military school texts; mixed authenticity debates—use for doctrine flavour, cross-check archaeology. |
| ***Shiji* 史記** (Sima Qian) | Qin unification wars; Zhao “Hu clothing & mounted archery” (*hufu qishe* 胡服騎射); early Han–Xiongnu; **court historiography**, not after-action reports. |
| ***Hanshu* 漢書** / ***Hou Hanshu* 後漢書** | Han institutions, Northern/Southern Armies, frontier policy; administrative bias. |
| **Qin terracotta army** (Lintong) | Life-size formation archaeology: infantry, kneeling/standing shooters, chariots, cavalry, command pit; bronze triggers, arrowheads, *ji*/*ge*/swords. Formation = funerary ideal, still best material TO&E proxy for late Qin. |
| **Yinqueshan Han slips** (Linyi, 1972) | *Sunzi*, *Sun Bin*, *Weiliaozi*, *Liutao*, “keeping ordinances” chapters—military library of an early Han officer. |
| **Juyan 居延 / Dunhuang frontier slips** | Han garrison logistics, watchtowers, crossbow inventories, signals—ground truth for frontier TD maps. |
| **Han armoury inventory (13 BCE)** | Extant counts of crossbows, bolts, *dao*, *jian*, spears, *ji*—scale of imperial stockpiles (see §1, §4). |

### Core modern scholarship (entry points)
Ralph D. **Sawyer** (trans./commentary on military classics); Mark Edward **Lewis**, *Sanctioned Violence in Early China*; Nicola **Di Cosmo**, *Ancient China and Its Enemies*; Edward L. **Shaughnessy** on early chariots; Robin D.S. **Yates** on Warring States/Qin military & crossbow; **Lorge**, *Chinese Martial Arts* / *War, Politics and Society*; Rafe **de Crespigny** on Later Han / Three Kingdoms military; Chun-shu **Chang** on Han Wu-di armies; Joseph **Needham**, *Science and Civilisation in China* V:6–7 (military tech / gunpowder); Xiuzhen **Li** / Marcos **Martinón-Torres** et al. on terracotta triggers & chromium myth (*Antiquity*; *Scientific Reports* 2019); Liang **Jieming**, *Chinese Siege Warfare*; Ian **Johnston**, *The Mozi: A Complete Translation*.

## 0. Framing: what “the Chinese army” was

**What it is.** Not one timeless institution. Rough phases for this brief:

1. **Shang–Western Zhou** — elite chariot-centred ritual warfare + retainers/levies.  
2. **Spring and Autumn** — chariot + growing infantry; multi-state aristocratic war.  
3. **Warring States** — mass conscript infantry, iron, crossbow doctrine, cavalry adoption, professional generals, siege science (Mohist).  
4. **Qin–Western Han** — imperial combined arms; capital professional cores; frontier cavalry wars vs steppe.  
5. **Eastern Han → Three Kingdoms** — decline of universal conscription; hereditary/personal armies; Romance ≠ history.  
6. **Tang / Song (brief TD enrichments only)** — *fubing* 府兵 military households; gunpowder incendiaries & early bombs—not Qing.

**Historical placement (primary focus).** Shang foundations through Qin–Han core, with Warring States depth; Three Kingdoms / Tang / Song only where they add TD-usable systems. **Qing is not primary.**

**Evidence quality.**
| Layer | Quality |
|---|---|
| Weapon typology (bronze → iron) | **High** (archaeology) |
| Crossbow triggers / mass production | **High** (terracotta metrology; Han slips) |
| Ideal city defence (Mozi) | **High text / medium practice** (prescriptive) |
| Unit sizes, ranks, drill | **Medium–low** (titles + estimates; numbers in histories often rhetorical) |
| Romance of the Three Kingdoms tactics | **Literary**, not primary history |

**Game-use potential:** Age unlocks — Shang/Zhou chariot aristocracy → Warring States mass infantry + *nu* towers → Qin terracotta combined-arms roster → Han frontier cavalry / convict / tribal auxiliaries → optional late unlocks (Three Kingdoms myth filter; Song gunpowder siege toys).

## 1. Infantry weapons and classes

### 1.1 Dagger-axe — *ge* 戈 (*gē*)

**What / kit.** Bronze (later iron) **dagger-axe / polearm**: transverse blade (*yuan* 援) mounted on a wooden shaft, typically with downward *hu* 胡 (“beard”) and tang (*nei* 內) for lashing. Designed for **hooking, pulling, and chopping**—especially from chariot decks against infantry—not primarily thrusting.

**Morphology timeline (archaeology).**
| Period | Form notes |
|---|---|
| Erlitou / early Shang | Early bronze *ge*; ritual jade *ge* also common |
| Mid–late Shang | Broader blades; tang evolves; bent-tang types appear terminal Shang |
| Western Zhou–Spring & Autumn | Long *hu*, multiple lashing holes; chariot-elite weapon |
| Warring States | Curved, more multi-role blades; then decline as mass infantry + cavalry rise |
| Qin–early Han | Bronze *ge* largely exits northern military mainstream; replaced by spear / *ji* / *dao* |

**Placement.** Shang–Zhou chariot warrior’s signature arm; Spring & Autumn still common; Warring States fades as close-order infantry and cavalry dominate.

**Sources.** Burial bronzes; Chiang / Wang Zhenhua typologies; Lorge; Shizhaishan comparative *ge* studies; terracotta weapon assemblage (few classic *ge* vs many long weapons).

**Controversies.**
1. “Sickle harvest from chariot” functional reading is **plausible reconstruction**, not a drill manual.  
2. Jade *ge* = **ritual/status**, not combat.  
3. Exact combat frequency vs display in late Zhou burials is debated.

**Game-use:** Early-age chariot-deck “hook” unit that pulls enemies off walls/lanes or disables shields; obsolete after cavalry unlock.

---

### 1.2 Spear and halberd — *mao* 矛 / *qiang* 槍 and *ji* 戟 (*jǐ*)

**What / kit.**
- **Spear / lance** (*mao*, later *qiang*): primary thrusting polearm of mass infantry once ranks thicken.  
- **Ji** (halberd): *ge* blade + spear point on one shaft (early: separate heads; later often combined casting). Enables thrust **and** hook/cut. Replaces standalone *ge* as standard versatile polearm by Han.

**Placement.** Warring States–Han line infantry backbone; terracotta warriors carry long bronze weapons (spears, lances, *ji*); Han inventory lists huge counts of long *pi* 鈹 / spears vs modest *ji* counts (specialist / prestige mix—do not over-read one list as universal TO&E).

**Sources.** Terracotta pits (Li / Martinón-Torres weapon studies); Han 13 BCE armoury table (*Hanshu*-era administrative tradition / excavated inventory tradition summarised in modern military surveys); Duan Jiong’s 167 CE formation description (halberds, swordsmen, spearmen, crossbows, light cavalry wings—de Crespigny).

**Controversies.** Modern English “halberd” for *ji* is approximate; multi-point bronze forms (*pi*, *tan*, etc.) blur categories. Depth of ranks (5 vs 10) varied by commander (*Shiji* anecdotes of Li Guang vs Cheng Buzhi).

**Game-use:** Core melee wall unit (*mao*); *ji* as anti-cavalry / hook+thrust upgrade with armour-pierce vs light horse.

---

### 1.3 Swords — *jian* 劍 (*jiàn*) and *dao* 刀 (*dāo*)

**What / kit.**
- **Jian**: double-edged straight sword; Warring States–Han prestige sidearm; fencing culture (*jian dao* manuals attested by title, mostly lost).  
- **Dao**: single-edged sabre; thickens spine; **cavalry-friendly**; ring-pommel *huanshou dao* 環首刀 common in Han. Gradually displaces *jian* as practical military sidearm.

**Placement.** Officers, dual-weapon infantry with shield, cavalry troopers; not the mass-primary arm (polearms + crossbows dominate numbers).

**Sources.** Han inventories (dao counts >> ceremonial *jian* heirloom counts in some tables); tomb finds; Mawangdui / regional sword archaeology; *Shiji*/*Hanshu* officer culture.

**Controversies.** “Horse-beheading *jian*” (*zhanma jian* 斬馬劍) — **execution / special blade lore**, not proven standard cavalry weapon. Popular wuxia overstates civilian sword ubiquity in field armies.

**Game-use:** Elite melee officers (*jian*); cavalry / anti-infantry slashers (*dao*); shield+*dao* as durable lane blockers.

### 1.4 Crossbow — *nu* 弩 (*nǔ*)

#### Trigger mechanism
**What.** Bronze **cast trigger** of interlocking pieces (commonly analysed as three working members + transverse bolts/pins): draws and holds high-tension string, releases on trigger pull **without a separate spring**. Pre-Han: trigger often set directly in wood stock; **Han improvement**: bronze casing (*guo* 郭) around mechanism + sometimes range scales on the housing—higher draw, better consistency (Lu / mechanical analyses; Needham tradition).

**Mass production.** Warring States–Qin triggers show **batch / cell workshop** production and high dimensional standardisation (terracotta trigger morphometrics: Li et al., *Antiquity*). Yates and others call interchangeable bronze triggers among the earliest **mass-produced complex military components**. Stack-mould / multi-cavity casting hypotheses apply especially to small bronze parts.

**Placement in formation (Qin archaeology).** Triggers + arrow bundles concentrate in **front ranks and flanks** of Pit 1; some shooters behind chariots in interior corridors—doctrine: long-range open fire before contact, then melee core.

#### Mass crossbow doctrine
**What.** Crossbow = less lifelong skill than composite bow; enables **levied infantry** to deliver heavy bolts with high penetration. Han frontier slips and capital inventories show crossbows as **standard**, not niche: e.g. armoury magnitudes on the order of **~500k+ crossbows** and **millions of bolts** in one late Western Han stocktake (figures transmitted via administrative lists—treat as order-of-magnitude imperial capacity, not a single battlefield).

**Qualification lore.** Training norms cited in later/traditional military writing: entry-level draw weights on the order of ~75+ kg; extreme “hands-and-feet” draws claimed far higher—**treat upper extremes as boasting / selection myths** unless tied to a specific slip.

**Volley practice.** Spring & Autumn texts already shift archery rhetoric toward **massed shooting**; Warring States–Han extend this with rotating ranks / screened shooters behind pavises (ideal descriptions in military books + tomb art of tower crossbowmen). Exact “three ranks kneeling/standing” choreography is partly **modern reconstruction** from terracotta poses + later manuals.

**Sources.** Terracotta triggers & arrowheads; Yinqueshan military corpus; Juyan slips; *Sunzi* (crossbow as metaphor **and** real weapon in period); Needham SCC; Yates 2007.

**Controversies.**
1. Invention date: triggers from ~6th c. BCE contexts (e.g. Qufu claims) vs literary firsts—**early Warring States maturation** is safe; “Sunzi invented it” is false.  
2. Effective range numbers in popular sources vary wildly—prefer “out-ranges contemporary hand bow in penetration at combat distances” over fake metre precision.  
3. **Chrome plating myth** for Qin bronze weapons: **debunked** (Martínón-Torres / Xiuzhen Li et al., *Scientific Reports* 2019)—surface Cr = lacquer contamination from organic parts; preservation ≈ alloy + soil chemistry, not ancient CCC coating.

**Game-use:** Signature Chinese tower weapon—slow reload, high damage, armour pierce; “workshop” building that buffs bolt supply; flank-tower placement bonus matching terracotta doctrine.

### 1.5 Repeating crossbow — *zhuge nu* 諸葛弩 / *lian nu* 連弩

**What.** Magazine-fed repeater combining spanning, loading, and release in a lever cycle. Popular name ties to **Zhuge Liang 諸葛亮**—**false as inventor**.

**Historicity.**
| Claim | Verdict |
|---|---|
| Zhuge Liang invented the handheld repeater | **Myth** (Romance / folk etymology) |
| Warring States Chu already had repeating designs | **Supported** — *Wu Yue Chunqiu* attributes invention to a Chu figure; archaeology: Qinjiazui Tomb 47 (Hubei), 4th c. BCE double-shot pistol-grip type |
| Zhuge improved **multiple-bolt** / large crossbow artillery | **Plausible textual tradition**—do not conflate with handheld *zhuge nu* |
| Handheld repeater = primary battlefield equaliser | **Overstated** — weak power; Ming texts say tip bolts with poison; often household / anti-bandit / naval niche; large mounted repeaters more “military” |

**Placement.** Defensive gates, walls, ships, civilian defence—**not** a replacement for standard *nu* in Han field armies.

**Sources.** Chu burial find; Needham; Loades; Ming *Wubei Zhi* / *Tiangong Kaiwu* reception history; Sawyer on Yang Xuan’s wagon-mounted automatic crossbows (180 CE—spectacular anecdote, single source cluster).

**Controversies.** Power vs rate-of-fire tradeoff; poison (aconite etc.) as force-multiplier = pharmacological claim needing caution in game fiction labelling.

**Game-use:** Low-damage rapid-fire wall gun with poison DoT upgrade; optional “Zhuge” skin clearly tagged **Romance branding**, unlock true Chu repeater earlier in tech tree.

## 2. Chariots then cavalry

### 2.1 Chariots — *zhanche* 戰車 (*zhànchē*)

**What.** Horse-drawn war chariot; typically **crew of three** in Zhou ideal: driver (*yu* 御), archer, striker with *ge*/*ji*. Prestige platform, command node (drums, bells, flags), and limited shock / pursuit on flat ground.

**Placement timeline.**
| Era | Role |
|---|---|
| Late Shang (~1250 BCE horses/chariots in China) | Elite package arriving via steppe–oasis transmission (Shaughnessy; Di Cosmo) |
| Western Zhou–Spring & Autumn | Peak aristocratic arm; state strength counted in “chariots” (*cheng* 乘) |
| Early Warring States | Still present; increasingly **command / support** as infantry masses grow |
| Late Warring States–Qin | Secondary to infantry + crossbow + emerging cavalry; terracotta still fields many chariots |
| Han | Rapid obsolescence vs Xiongnu mobility; lingering defensive / ceremonial use into 2nd–1st c. BCE |

**Combined-arms company ideal (Zhou reconstruction from *Zuo* institutional notes / later systematising texts).** Often cited pattern: chariot + ~72 infantry in three platoons + supply cart—**reconstructed model**, useful for game org charts, not a single excavated roster.

**Limitations.** Needs flat, open terrain; wide turning circle; expensive horses/crew—poor vs broken ground, forests, fortifications, and steppe horse-archers.

**Sources.** Shang–Zhou vehicle pits; *Zuo Zhuan*; Shaughnessy 1988; terracotta chariots (Pit 1–2); *Shiji* on Han campaigns.

**Controversies.** “Chariot = ancient tank ramming” vs “mobile archery + command”—Chinese evidence favours **missile/command + limited contact**, not scythed Persian-style fantasy.

**Game-use:** Early mobile tower / buff aura (drums = reload/morale buff); banned from mountain/forest lanes; sunsets when cavalry tech completes.

---

### 2.2 Cavalry rise — *qibing* 騎兵 (*qíbīng*)

**What.** Mounted combat, especially **mounted archery**, adopted under northern (*Hu* 胡) influence.

**Key political marker.** King **Wuling of Zhao** 趙武靈王 (r. late 4th c. BCE): reforms **“Hu clothing and mounted shooting”** (*hufu qishe* 胡服騎射, trad. 307 BCE)—trousers/short coats/boots + cavalry archery. *Shiji* “Zhao hereditary house.” Court resistance = cultural drama; military result = Zhao northern expansion, incorporation of Loufan / Linhu horse peoples as auxiliaries.

**Han maturation.** Early Han horse stocks limited; pastures and breeding under Emperors Wen–Jing–Wu expand herds into the hundreds of thousands (administrative claims). Vs **Xiongnu** 匈奴, cavalry becomes decisive; infantry–crossbow armies without enough horse cannot dictate tempo. Single mounting aids appear; **true twin stirrups** later (post-Han / Jin developments)—do not give Han cataphracts modern saddle tech.

**Placement.** Flankers, pursuit, frontier columns, raids; terracotta Pit 2 already shows cavalry with distinct dress (pillbox-type hats, etc.).

**Sources.** *Shiji* Zhao & Xiongnu chapters; Di Cosmo; Han pasture policy; terracotta cavalry; Juyan horse/kit slips.

**Controversies.** “Wuling invented Chinese cavalry” is **too neat**—mounted fighting appears earlier in border zones; Wuling = **state institutionalisation** moment. Numbers of Han cavalry in modern secondary literature (80k–300k) are **estimates** from campaign scales + fiscal models (Chang)—flag as such.

**Game-use:** Mid-game unlock requiring “Hu dress” research; high mobility anti-siege-engine hunters; weak in prolonged wall slugfests without infantry screen.

## 3. Elite / special troops

### 3.1 Qin terracotta troop types (archaeological roster)

**What (pits).**
| Pit | Content (summary) |
|---|---|
| **Pit 1** | Main infantry formation; armoured & unarmoured infantry; standing / kneeling archers–crossbowmen; chariots; officers |
| **Pit 2** | Combined detachment: cavalry, chariots, infantry—often read as a “guard / reaction” force |
| **Pit 3** | Command post: officers + chariot |
| **Pit 4** | Empty / unfinished |

**Figure types.** Generals (taller), lower officers, armoured infantry, unarmoured infantry, kneeling armoured shooters, standing unarmoured shooters, chariot drivers (heavier armour), cavalrymen, horses. Hairstyles, armour lamellae patterns, and footwear vary by role—**mass customisation from mould modules**, not 8,000 unique portraits in the romantic sense.

**Weapons assemblage.** 40,000+ arrowheads (often ~100/quiver bundles), hundreds of triggers, swords, spears/lances, *ji*, hooks, ferrules, ceremonial *shu* 殳. Organic stocks/shafts mostly gone.

**Sources.** SIAATQ excavation reports; Li et al. trigger study; *Archaeology International* weapons overview; UCL–Terracotta Army collaborative papers.

**Controversies.** Formation = afterlife ideal army mirroring Qin practice **in broad arms mix**, not a literal order of battle for a named campaign. Polychromy originally bright—modern grey aesthetic is misleading for art direction if “historical palette” is a goal.

**Game-use:** Faction roster blueprint—kneeling *nu* towers, spear core, chariot buff wagons, cavalry flankers, general aura units.

---

### 3.2 Han professional capital armies — Northern & Southern

**Northern Army** (*beijun* 北軍): professional force guarding capital perimeter (from 180 BCE). Eastern Han reorg (~31–39 CE): five colonels (*xiaowei* 校尉)—e.g. garrison cavalry (*tunji* 屯騎), picked cavalry (*yueji* 越騎), infantry (*bubing* 步兵), Chang River (*changshui* 長水), archers who “shoot at sound” (*shesheng* 射聲)—on the order of ~900 each in one tabulated scheme (~4,200 total).  

**Southern Army** (*nanjun* 南軍): palace guard focus (trad. established 138 BCE; size figures vary by period/source—**do not freeze one number**).

**Other capital elites.** *Huben* 虎賁 (“Rapid as Tigers”), *Yulin* 羽林 (“Feathered Forest”—often sons/grandsons of the fallen), city-gate colonel, Bearer of the Gilded Mace (*zhijinwu* 執金吾) police-cavalry/halberdiers.

**Sources.** *Hanshu* / *Hou Hanshu* treatises & tables; modern summaries (Bielenstein tradition; Wikipedia synthesis mirroring standard sinology—verify against Twitchett/Loewe *Cambridge History* where possible).

**Game-use:** Expensive unique towers/heroes unlocked only on capital maps; *shesheng* as night/fog shooting specialists.

---

### 3.3 Frontier cavalry, convict soldiers, tribal auxiliaries

**Frontier.** Long-service garrisons, military-agricultural colonies; meticulous Juyan paperwork (signals, archery tests, grain/salt rations, equipment issues)—TD gold for “supply integrity” mechanics.

**Convict / low-status troops.** Commutation of sentence via frontier service; Emperor Wu-era mass use of paroled convicts, pardoned criminals, “men of bad reputation,” merchants under exile categories, etc., especially in southern/southwestern and hard campaigns (Chang’s quantitative reading of *Shiji*/*Hanshu* campaign notes). Professionalisation shifts social image of the soldier downward even as officer elites gain power.

**Tribal auxiliaries.** Resettled Xiongnu factions, Wuhuan, Qiang, southern Yue forces, etc.—especially Eastern Han cavalry heavily dependent on non-Han horsemen. Zhao Wuling already absorbed northern tribal horse units.

**Sources.** *Shiji* Xiongnu / Nan Yue; *Hanshu* geography & biographies; Juyan corpus; Di Cosmo; de Crespigny.

**Controversies.** “Barbarian cavalry saved Han” vs “Han organisational logistic core enabled barbarian cavalry”—both partial; game should show **dependency + risk of defection**.

**Game-use:** Cheap disposable convict wave clearers with desertion risk; powerful auxiliary cavalry with loyalty meter; frontier watchtower module copying Juyan signal drill.

## 4. Command and organisation

### 4.1 *Jun* 軍 / *shi* 師 and lower structures

**What is attested.**
- ***Jun* 軍** — “army” as a large formation / army-group concept; also in place-names and institutional titles (e.g. Northern Army).  
- ***Shi* 師** — “division / host” in classical military vocabulary; Zhou–Warring States schematic pyramids in later systematising texts.  
- Common decimal flavour in many periods: **wu 伍 (5)** → larger multiples (*shi* 什, *dui* 隊, *tun* 屯, etc.)—exact ladder **varies by state and century**.

**Zhou–Spring & Autumn schematic (commonly cited reconstruction).** Companies of ~100; battalions; *shi*; multi-*jun* field forces—useful for game, but **not a universal excavated org chart**.

**Qin–Han campaign chain (typical titles).** *Jiangjun* 將軍 (general, often ad hoc appointment) → lieutenant-general / commandant → *xiaowei* 校尉 (colonel) → *sima* 司馬 (major) → *junhou* 軍候 → *tunzhang* 屯長 (platoon chief). **No permanent Napoleonic general staff**—generals appointed from court as needed; failure could mean death.

**Sources.** *Zuo Zhuan*; military classics; *Shiji* appointment narratives; Han tables; Tang *fubing* later decimal echoes (*tuan* / *dui* / *huo*).

**Controversies.** Forcing one “Chinese legion TO&E” across 1,000 years is **designer fiction**—present as era toggles.

**Game-use:** Tech tree renames (wu → tun → bu → jun) that raise command radius; losing the general collapses aura buffs (historical risk of over-centralised command).

---

### 4.2 Generals and military theory culture

Professionalisation of generalship is a **Warring States** hallmark (Sun Bin, Bai Qi, Lian Po, Wang Jian, etc.—layered in *Shiji*). Merit, deception, logistics, and terrain outrank aristocratic chariot prowess.

**Bamboo-text reality check.** Yinqueshan shows early Han officers studying *Sunzi* **and** *Sun Bin* side by side—game “codex” items should be plural, not a single sacred book.

## 5. Combined arms; steppe & siege

### 5.1 Combined arms (Qin–Han ideal)

Crossbowmen/archers forward & flanks → infantry & *ji*/spear centre → chariots as command/shock residue → cavalry wings for encircle/pursuit. Confirmed in broad outline by terracotta layout and by Later Han tactical snapshots (e.g. Duan Jiong’s layered halberd/sword/spear + crossbow + light cavalry wings).

**Signals.** Flags, drums, gongs/bells—Mozi and military books obsess over signalling; mis-signal = friendly chaos (game mechanic).

---

### 5.2 Vs steppe nomads (Xiongnu and predecessors)

**Han problem set.** Higher strategic mobility of horse-archers; Chinese conscript infantry slow to train as riders; logistics of deep steppe campaigns crushing (grain/salt cart maths in Zhao Chongguo memorials).

**Han answers.** Horse breeding; long-service professionals; allied tribal cavalry; crossbow volume; walls/beacon lines; diplomatic split-and-settle (*heqin* 和親 then offensive under Wu).

**Strengths.** Logistics bureaucracy, mass missile production, siege/engineering when enemy can be fixed, depth of manpower.  
**Weaknesses.** Cavalry quality/quantity early on; overextended supply; political cost of convict/auxiliary reliance.

**Game-use:** Steppe enemy faction with mobility cheat; Han player must build horse pastures + beacon towers or lose initiative.

---

### 5.3 Siege warfare

Rammed-earth walls resist mining better than stone sapping stereotypes but **fear water**. Attack options catalogued by Mohists (below). Field armies without siege trains stall at walled cities—Warring States political map = fortress puzzle.

**Game-use:** Separate “open battle lanes” vs “city wall lanes” with different unit caps.

## 6. *Art of War* practical takeaways for TD (not fortune cookies)

Map *Sunzi* (and where noted *Sun Bin*) to **systems**, not quotes.

| Principle (paraphrase) | TD implementation |
|---|---|
| Know terrain / “ground” types | Lane biomes change unit efficiency (chariot nerf in mud/hills) |
| Deception & empty-full | Decoy towers; fog of war; fake weak gates (Mozi hanging-fire trap synergy) |
| Speed & opportunity windows | Timed sally buttons; buff windows after enemy fatigue |
| Logistics decide campaigns | Ammo/food metres; interrupted wagon path = army collapse |
| Avoid prolonged hopeless siege | Victory condition: hold until attacker supply breaks—not only “kill all” |
| Spies | Reveal enemy wave composition next round |
| Fire as force multiplier (ch. 12) | See §7—fire must be followed by assault or wasted |
| Water isolates more than destroys | Flood slows/splits enemies; rare instant kill |
| *Sun Bin*: crossbow / siege / cavalry modernity | Mid-game tech trio required for empire-tier defence |

**Avoid:** mystical “ancient Chinese wisdom” loading screens; attribute lines to **text + era**.

## 7. Fire, flood, ambush, crossbow volleys

### 7.1 Fire attacks — *huogong* 火攻

**Sunzi** distinguishes targets: personnel, stores, baggage, arsenals, formations—and stresses weather/wind and **immediate follow-up**. Historical practice: burning gates, fire arrows, heated sand/metal (Mozi), later gunpowder incendiaries (Song).

**Game-use:** Fire DoT on gates/engines; wind direction RNG; if no melee push within N seconds, enemy extinguishes and gains rage buff (punish passive turtling).

### 7.2 Flooding — *shuigong* 水攻

Divert rivers / dikes against rammed-earth walls (Warring States norm). Defenders dig drainage / counter-channels; fight on coupled boats (*Mozi* “approachers”). Famous narrative floods exist in histories—**verify case-by-case** before naming a scenario “historical.”

**Game-use:** Map hazard that damages walls but also drowns attackers in low lanes; engineer unit toggles sluices.

### 7.3 Ambush — *fuzji* / *qi* 奇

Irregular (*qi*) vs regular (*zheng* 正) forces—classic pair. Feigned retreat vs Xiongnu is dangerous (they do it better). City defence analogue: concealed drop-bridges, sally ports, night sorties (Mozi).

**Game-use:** One-time trap tiles; sally cooldown; “empty city” bluff (literary—label Romance if using Zhuge’s empty fort).

### 7.4 Crossbow volleys

Opening saturation from walls/front ranks; rotating shooters; pavise screens; tower ballistae (*joined crossbow carriages* in Mozi—windlass, multi-crew).

**Game-use:** Staggered volley meter; overwatch cones from flank towers matching terracotta doctrine.

## 9. Mohist defensive science — city defence checklist (*Mozi*)

> Prescriptive corpus (*Bei chengmen* 備城門 and related). Surviving chapters imperfect; treat as **engineer’s ideal**. Translation base: Ian Johnston; Chinese Text Project for chapter map.

### 9.1 Twelve attack types to prepare against

Listed to Qin Guli 禽滑釐: **lin 臨** (high approach), **gou 鉤** (hooks), **chong 衝** (rams), **ti 梯** (ladders), **yin 堙** (mounds), **shui 水** (water), **xue 穴** (tunnels), **tu 突** (sudden rush), **kongdong 空洞** (obscure—“empty cavern”; meaning uncertain—**flag gap**), **yifu 蟻傅** (“ant” swarm climb), **fenwen 轒轀** (armoured assault wagon / “tank”), **gaoche / related high carts**.

**Game-use:** Twelve enemy wave modifiers; each requires a different tower module.

### 9.2 Pre-siege checklist (condensed)

**Logistics & politics**
- [ ] Grain & firewood stocked; weapons repaired  
- [ ] Clear fields of fire: demolish cover within bowshot  
- [ ] Poison/fill external wells; deny shelter  
- [ ] Evacuate surplus population if city too crowded  
- [ ] Secure neighbouring relief alliances (*Mozi* stresses help may still fail without skill)  
- [ ] Appoint Defender (inside) & Director (outside)  

**Wall & gate hardware**
- [ ] Portcullis / hanging gate (*xuanmen* 縣門) with winch  
- [ ] Iron-bound bars/bolts; earth coating on doors vs fire  
- [ ] Water pour-holes above gates vs fire arrows  
- [ ] Furnaces, bellows, pipes for smoke/fire killbox  
- [ ] Moat with stakes; camouflaged liftable bridge  
- [ ] Double parapets with spiked trench between  
- [ ] Corner observation towers; interval towers by function  
- [ ] Palisades + multiple stake rows beyond wall  

**Detection & counter-sap**
- [ ] Buried jars / geophones; dogs; sharp-eared listeners  
- [ ] Counter-mines; smoke/poison bellows into enemy tunnels  

**Artillery & ammo**
- [ ] Traction trebuchets / throwing arms with stone piles  
- [ ] Caltrops, tiles, thunder stones  
- [ ] Movable furnaces: boiling water, sand, molten metal  
- [ ] Joined crossbow carriages + screens  
- [ ] Moving platforms higher than wall to beat cloud ladders  

**Human organisation**
- [ ] Women on wall with spears; men majority crossbows (ideal ratio in text)  
- [ ] Tallies by day, passwords by night  
- [ ] Five-man mutual responsibility squads  
- [ ] Crack troops for timed sallies (hostage leverage on families—**historical harshness**; tone carefully in game)  
- [ ] Rewards for wall defence; brutal penalties for collusion  

**Active defence doctrine.** “Harm the enemy swiftly”; do not only stall—sally, trap, counter-engineer. Prolonged pure passive defence is second-best if relief is uncertain.

## 10. Brief later enrichments (not primary eras)

### 10.1 Three Kingdoms — history vs Romance

| Romance (*Sanguo yanyi* 三國演義) | History (*Sanguozhi* 三國志 + peers) |
|---|---|
| Zhuge invents repeating crossbow / godlike magic tactics | Zhuge = logistics/administration/northern expeditions; inventions inflated |
| Empty Fort Stratagem as factual | Literary anecdote tradition |
| Personal duels decide wars | Forces, supply, fortifications, defections |
| Endless immortal generals | Attrition, epidemic, famine |

**Game-use:** Optional “Romance Mode” skin pack; default bible stays *Sanguozhi*-sober.

### 10.2 Tang *fubing*

Farmer-soldier garrisons; rotational service; fails under perpetual frontier war → professionalisation. Good for “militia regenerates unless wars never end” mechanic.

### 10.3 Song gunpowder (TD spice)

*Wujing zongyao* 武經總要 (1044): formulas, fire arrows, gunpowder pots. **Thunderclap bombs** (*pili pao*) at Kaifeng 1126—panic/concussion more than modern HE. Fire-lances; later iron bombs. Optional late tech—not a Qin default.
