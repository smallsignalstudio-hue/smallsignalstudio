# ACHAEMENID PERSIAN MILITARY UNITS AND TACTICS — Research Brief (Tower-Defence Game Doc)

> English research notes for a later Turkish audience. Terms given in **Latin / Greek / reconstructed Old Persian** where attested or conventionally reconstructed.  
> **Method:** Prefer primary sources; flag uncertainty and scholarly conflict explicitly. Do **not** treat popular reconstructions (Osprey plates, wargame lists) as primary evidence.

## 0. Framing: what “the Persian army” was

**What it is.** Not a single national army in the Greek city-state sense, but an imperial levy (*kara* / Greek *stratos*) built around (1) an ethnic Persian–Median core, (2) satrapal and subject contingents under their own officers but Persian high command, (3) a royal household / palatine force around the king, and (4)—especially from the later 5th century—large numbers of Greek mercenaries.

**Historical placement.** Teispid–Achaemenid empire c. 550–330 BCE. Peak descriptive evidence for “classic” archery–shield-wall infantry: Greco-Persian Wars (490–479). Peak evidence for late reforms / *kardakes* / scythed chariots vs Macedon: 401–330.

**Evidence quality.** High for *existence* of multi-ethnic levies and decimal command language in Greek; low for Iranian unit titles, exact TO&E, and battlefield diagrams. Iconography (Persepolis “guards”) cannot be securely equated with named literary corps.

**Game-use potential:** Empire as a “faction of factions”—unlockable ethnic contingents with different kit and doctrine, not one homogeneous Persian infantry blob.

## 1. Core infantry: *sparabara*, *takabara*, *cardaces* / *kardakes*

### 1.1 Sparabara (OPers. reconstructed *spara-bara-* “shield-bearers”; Greek *γέρρα* for the shields)

**What it is.** Conventional modern label for the early–mid Achaemenid Persian (and Median-style) line infantry doctrine: large rectangular/tall **wicker–leather pavises** (*spara*; Gk. *γέρρα*) planted as a **shield wall**, with massed archers firing from behind, then close combat with short spears and sidearms (*akinakēs*).

**Historical placement.** Dominant picture for the Persian Wars generation (Marathon–Plataea–Mykale). Likely adapting older Near Eastern (esp. Neo-Assyrian) archer-behind-shield practice, but with a higher archer-to-shield ratio in modern reconstructions.

**Equipment (what sources actually say).**
- Herodotus 7.61 (Persians): soft felt *tiara*; sleeved embroidered tunic; iron scale armour “like fish scales”; trousers; **wicker shields**; quivers; **short spears**; **powerful bows** with **reed arrows**; daggers at the right thigh.
- Herodotus 9.61–62, 9.102 (Plataea, Mykale): Persians form a **barrier of wicker shields** (*γέρρα*) and shoot; when the wall is broken, hand-to-hand follows.
- Iconography: tall rectangular shields and “figure-8” / violin shields appear in Achaemenid art; **no single image shows one man with full Herodotus kit (spara + bow + spear + scale)**—so Hdt. 7.61 may be an **inventory of the corps’ arsenal**, not every man’s personal loadout.

**Tactical role (reconstructed).**
- Modern consensus reconstruction (Sekunda et al.): file of ~10 (*dathabam*): **1 spara-bearer** in front, **~9 archers** behind → maximize volume of fire per shield-front.
- Alternative reading: more men may have carried *spara* (if taking Hdt. 7.61 literally); evidence quality drops here.
- Doctrine: disrupt with arrows → plant/hold wall → either hold under missile cover or charge after disruption (Kambouris & Bakas 2022 argue for Roman-like “missile then shock,” not pure static defence).

**Sources.** Hdt. 7.61; 9.61–63; 9.102; Assyrian antecedents in art; Greek vase paintings of Persians with oblong shields; Sekunda 1989/1992; Konijnendijk on Plataea; Kambouris & Bakas 2022.

**Controversies.**
1. **Term *sparabara*:** Modern scholarly/wargaming reconstruction from OPers. *spara* + *-bara*; **not** securely attested as an official unit title in Achaemenid texts.
2. **Armour at Plataea:** Hdt. 9.62–63 calls Persians fighting Spartans effectively “naked” / unequipped relative to hoplites—yet 7.61 gives scale armour. Resolutions include: (a) shields grounded as wall so men fought “without shields in hand”; (b) only front ranks armoured; (c) rhetorical Greek contrast; (d) different troop categories in successive lines (standing archers vs armoured veterans—see Xen. *Cyr.* 1.2.13).
3. **1:9 ratio:** Influential, not proven by a primary TO&E document.
4. Persepolis “Immortal” look-alikes often lack the large *spara*—ceremonial vs field kit uncertain.

**Game-use potential:** Classic “pavise wall” tower/emplacement unit—high ranged DPS while the wall stands; collapses into fragile melee when the *spara* line is broken (Plataea failure state).

### 1.2 Takabara (OPers. reconstructed *taka-bara-* “bearers of the *taka*”)

**What it is.** Modern label for infantry shown with **crescent / scalloped light shields** (often compared to Greek *πέλτη*) and fighting more as **mobile shield-and-spear / javelin troops** than as *spara*-wall archers.

**Historical placement.** Especially associated by Sekunda with **later 5th–4th century** western imperial practice and **garrison** roles; crescent shields appear more in later Greek battle art and western contexts. Whether they are “reformed sparabara,” ethnic subject troops, or Xenophon’s armoured **veteran reserves** is debated.

**Equipment.** Crescent wicker/wood light shield; spears and/or javelins; sometimes axe (*sagaris*) in popular descriptions—**axe link is shaky** if generalized beyond Sacae/Scythians. Lighter kit than hoplite *aspis* + *dory*.

**Sources.** Primarily **iconographic** identification + Sekunda 1992; Xen. *Cyr.* 1.2.13 (veterans with armour and close-combat kit—**not** named *takabara*); Greek pottery. **No** Herodotus passage uses the word.

**Controversies.**
- Name and typology are **Sekunda-era reconstructions**; evidence quality **lower** than for *spara*-wall tactics described by Herodotus.
- Kambouris & Bakas: possible identity with **mobilized reserve** close-combat infantry (cuirassed), not merely garrison peltasts.
- Wikipedia-style claims (“easily defeated by hoplites,” “from Iraq/Iran”) overstate certainty.

**Game-use potential:** Mid-game “peltast” / flexible infantry—faster than *sparabara*, worse under arrow-storm defence, better at flanking towers and broken terrain.

### 1.3 Cardaces / Kardakes (Gk. *κάρδακες*; Latinized *cardaces*)

**What it is.** A late-appearing infantry designation in Greek sources, most famously Arrian’s **60,000 *kardakes*** at Issus (333 BCE), described in terms that suggest **heavy / hoplite-like** infantry flanking Greek mercenaries (*Anab.* 2.8.6). Strabo 15.3.18 connects *kardakes* with Persian youth training (etymologies offered in antiquity: “manly,” “robbers”—unreliable).

**Historical placement.** Secure literary spotlight: **late Achaemenid** (Datames in Nepos; Issus in Arrian/Polybius–Callisthenes tradition). **Not** a clear category in Herodotus’s Persian Wars narrative.

**Equipment / role — conflicting traditions.**
- **Arrian:** *kardakes* as (quasi-)hoplites at Issus.
- **Callisthenes** (via Polybius): Persian *peltastai* at Issus—lighter reading.
- **Charles 2012:** ethnically diverse **general-purpose infantry** akin to Iphicratean peltasts; dismisses Strabo as weak evidence for early institutional meaning; notes disappearance of “Immortals” from the late literary record as context for seeking a replacement label.
- **Kambouris & Bakas 2022:** socio-military class of trained Persian draftees—first as *sparabara* standing troops (~ages ~16–25 / 10-year service in Xenophon’s scheme), then as armoured CQB reserves; later **rearmed toward hoplite standard** under Darius III (possibly influenced by Charidemus)—a coherent but **speculative** synthesis.

**Sources.** Arr. *Anab.* 2.8.6; Polyb. 12.17ff. (Callisthenes); Nep. *Datames* 8; Strabo 15.3.18–19; Xen. *Cyr.* 1.2 (age grades—**without** the word *kardakes*); Charles 2012.

**Controversies.** Light vs heavy; mercenary vs native; whether the word is a troop type or a social/training category; whether Darius III’s “hoplitization” is real reform or Greek interpretive overlay. **Evidence quality: medium for late existence, low for continuous identity back to 480.**

**Game-use potential:** Late-tech “reformed infantry” unlock—trade arrow-cloud doctrine for anti-hoplite melee; historically themed as risky modernization (Issus underperformance).

## 2. The Immortals (*Athánatoi* / Anusiya / *Amṛtaka*)

### 2.1 What Herodotus says

**Name.** Greek *Ἀθάνατοι* (“Immortals”). Herodotus explains: the corps was **always kept at 10,000**—if a man died or fell sick, he was replaced at once (7.83.1).

**Placement in Xerxes’ host.** After listing six infantry army-corps commanders (7.82), Hdt. adds Hydarnes (OPers. *Vidṛna-*) son of Hydarnes as commander of the 10,000 Immortals (7.83). At Thermopylae they are committed and fail frontally, then turn the pass via the Anopaia path (7.211). At 8.113 Mardonius supposedly keeps the Immortals for 479—but they vanish from the 479 narrative; **Hignett and Schmitt** think they likely **returned to Asia with Xerxes** as royal escort, and 8.113 may be wrong.

**Equipment / display.**
- Native Persians “best and most magnificently equipped”; abundant gold; wagons with concubines/servants; separate food on camels/mules (7.83.2)—Schmitt notes it is **not certain** this luxury paragraph attaches only to Immortals vs Persians generally.
- Field kit for Persians generally: 7.61 (above).
- March from Sardis (7.40–41): **10,000 picked Persian foot**; of these **1,000** with spears tipped at the butt with **golden pomegranates** (*ῥοιαί*), surrounding **9,000** with **silver** pomegranates. Scholars usually link this picked 10,000 to the Immortals, though Hdt. does not say *Athánatoi* in 7.41.

### 2.2 Iranian name — major controversy

| Proposal | Meaning | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Confusion of OPers. *anušiya-* “companions / followers” with *anauša-* “immortal” | Explains Greek name as mistranslation | Pagliaro; popularized (Livius). **Schmitt / Gnoli:** serious linguistic and semantic reservations—problem **unsolved**. |
| Reconstructed *Amṛtaka* / *Amrtaka* “immortals” | Matches Greek sense | **Sekunda speculation**; Schmitt: “mere speculation,” not attested. |
| Herodotus’s functional explanation (constant 10k) | Literal Greek etiology | Possible folk etymology even if Iranian name differed. |

**Iranica (Schmitt):** essential questions—origin, exact tasks, Iranian name—**cannot be solved** because authentic Iranian sources do not name the corps.

### 2.3 Size, recruitment, status

- **10,000:** Only Herodotus’s figure for this named corps; fits decimal ideology (*myrias*). Whether always literally full is unknowable.
- **Recruitment:** “Select Persians” (and wider Iranian participation often assumed from art: Persians, Medes, Elamites in “guard” dress). No tablet roster labelled Immortals.
- **Status:** Elite infantry of the royal army; Hydarnes’ command; possible overlap with—but not identical to—the innermost **apple-bearer** guard (see §9). Charles 2011: Immortals better as **palatine infantry**, while **μηλοφόροι** + kinsmen cavalry are the true bodyguard pair.
- **Did they fight “as described”?** Thermopylae: yes, they are combat troops, not parade only. Frontal failure in the pass shows limits of their kit/formation in constrained melee vs spear-phalanx. Gold pomp is compatible with elite status but may be literary colour.

**Sources.** Hdt. 7.41, 7.83, 7.211, 8.113; Athenaeus 12.514c (Heracleides of Cumae—derivative); later Greek/Roman echoes; Persepolis/Susa guard reliefs (**not proven Immortals**); Schmitt, *Iranica* “Immortals”; Charles 2011; Briant 2002.

**Controversies.** Name; whether Susa brick guards = Immortals; relation to apple-bearers; whether they existed as a continuous institution into Alexander’s day (Alexander historians prefer *μηλοφόροι*); decimal titles *baivarabam* / *baivarapatiš* are reconstructions.

**Game-use potential:** Premium regenerating elite (Herodotus’s “never under strength”)—expensive unique infantry with gold/silver spear variants as cosmetic ranks; strong but not invincible in chokepoints (Thermopylae lesson).

## 3. Archers: mass archery doctrine, composite bows, *spara* wall

**What it is.** Archery is the **signature Persian infantry offensive method** in Greek narratives of the Wars: soften, disorder, pin—then close or let cavalry work.

**Bow.** “Powerful bows” and **reed arrows** (Hdt. 7.61). Near Eastern **composite** construction is the standard modern technical assumption for Achaemenid Iranian bows (horn/sinew/wood); Herodotus does not write an engineering treatise. Xenophon (*Anab.* 3.3–4) notes Persian archery vs Rhodian slingers and Cretan high-angle shooting—implies practical limits and mostly **flatter trajectories** in some readings (Kambouris).

**Doctrine.**
- Volume fire from depth behind *spara* (Plataea: continuous shooting until Greeks close).
- Famous anecdote (Hdt. 7.226): arrows darkening the sun—literary hyperbole that still encodes **massed missile** self-image.
- Cavalry also shoots / javelins; Sacae are specialist horse-archers (below).

**Pavise wall.** At Plataea and Mykale the *γέρρα* wall is the archer’s firebase. Once breached by hoplites, archers in rear ranks are at a severe disadvantage if caught before relief by better melee troops.

**Success conditions.** Best in open approaches, sieges, against loosely ordered or shield-poor enemies, and when cavalry prevents clean closure. **Worst** when forced into narrow frontal grind vs unbroken spear-walls (Marathon tradition; Thermopylae front; final Plataea push).

**Controversies.** How “composite” vs self bows in each contingent (Indians’ reed bows in Hdt. 7.65 may differ); angle of fire; whether all rear-rankers also carried spears for the charge (Hdt. implies sidearms; spears for all is partly reconstructive).

**Game-use potential:** Core “arrow storm” ability tied to intact shield-wall buff; counters = rush tactics, smoke/terrain, or breaking the pavise line.

## 4. Cavalry and shock mounts

### 4.1 Persian and Median cavalry

**What.** Horse-borne nobility and levied horsemen; in Xerxes’ list, Persians, Medes, Kissians among cavalry peoples (Hdt. 7.84–86). Equipment “like the infantry” for many, with some metal helmets (7.84).

**Role.** Flanking, pursuit, missile harassment, exploiting disorder; at Plataea, Persian cavalry (Masistius) is dangerous until checked. In *Anabasis*, Persian cavalry fight with **palta** (javelin-spears)—bows less prominent than Greek stereotype expects for late 5th c. western theatres.

**Numbers.** Hdt. 7.87: **80,000** cavalry (plus camels/chariots)—almost certainly schematic/inflated.

### 4.2 Bactrian and other East Iranian horse

Bactrians in infantry kit with reed bows and short spears (7.64); cavalry “equipped as their foot” (7.86). Parthians, Chorasmians, Sogdians, Gandarians, etc., share Bactrian-style equipment in the infantry catalogue (7.66)—useful as **steppe-adjacent missile cavalry/infantry** pool for game factions.

### 4.3 Sacae / Scythians (OPers. *Sakā*; Gk. *Σάκαι*)

**What.** “Persians call all Scythians Sacae” (Hdt. 7.64). Amyrgian Sacae: pointed stiff caps, trousers, native bows, daggers, battle-axe ***sagaris***. Elite reputation as horse-archers in wider Iranian military history; on ships as marines with Persians and Medes (7.96).

**Game-use potential:** Fast harasser / hit-and-run unit; axe as anti-armour meme weapon (use lightly—don’t invent “Sacae destroy hoplites” without caveats).

### 4.4 Camel corps (Cyrus vs Croesus)

**Herodotus 1.80 (Battle of Sardis / Thymbra tradition):** Cyrus places **camels** (baggage camels re-roled) opposite Lydian **cavalry**; Lydian horses **cannot stand the sight/smell of camels**; cavalry breaks; Cyrus wins. Arab camel-riders appear in Xerxes’ army (7.86–87), posted **rear** so as not to frighten horses.

**Xenophon *Cyropaedia* 7.1:** Camels used in the didactic battle narrative—**not independent confirmation** of 547 BCE tactics.

**Evidence quality.** Camel vs horse fright: recurring ancient motif; Hdt. is the main “historical” citation for Cyrus–Croesus. Treat as **famous stratagem with plausible animal-behaviour core**, not as a standing Achaemenid “camel knight” arm.

**Game-use potential:** Situational anti-cavalry debuff unit / one-shot stratagem relic (“Cyrus’s camels”).

### 4.5 Scythed chariots (*ἅρματα δρεπανηφόρα*)

**What.** Chariots with scythe blades on axles (and variants under the box)—designed to break infantry formations.

**Attestations.**
- **Invention myths:** Xen. *Cyr.* 6.1.27ff. credits Cyrus; Ctesias/Diodorus push fantasies into deep antiquity—**unreliable**.
- **First solid field use in Greek narrative:** Cunaxa 401 (Xen. *Anab.* 1.8)—blades from axles and under the seat; both sides said to have them in the tradition; limited clear impact in Xenophon’s own fight narrative.
- **Gaugamela 331:** Darius III fields large numbers (Arr.; Diod. 17.53; Curt.); Alexander’s army opens lanes, missiles kill teams; some damage occurs where gaps fail—**mixed effectiveness**.
- Nefiodkin and others: possibly developed mid-5th c. for wars in open terrain (e.g. vs Egyptian–Athenian forces)—hypothesis, not certainty.

**Effectiveness (scholarly caution).** Spectacular vs dense, inflexible crowds; **poor** vs disciplined troops who open ranks and shoot the teams. Half of anecdotal cases “work,” half fail (Book and Sword / Nefiodkin discussion).

**Game-use potential:** High-risk charge ability that devastates clustered melee blobs but is soft-countered by formation split / spike traps / focused tower fire on horses.

## 5. Navy: Phoenician, Egyptian, Ionian contingents; triremes; Salamis

**What it is.** An **imperial fleet levied from maritime subjects**, not a “Persian” national navy. Persians/Medes/Sacae serve as **marines** on the ships (Hdt. 7.96).

### Contingents (Hdt. 7.89–95) — selected
| Supplier | Triremes (Hdt.) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Phoenicians + Syrians of Palestine | 300 | Best sailors; Sidonians foremost (7.96) |
| Egyptians | 200 | Sea spears, great axes, cuirasses |
| Cypriotes | 150 | Mixed Greek/Phoenician/Ethiopian origin traditions |
| Cilicians | 100 | |
| Ionians | 100 | Greek kit; politically suspect loyalty |
| Hellespontine Greeks | 100 | |
| Carians | 70 | |
| Aeolians | 60 | |
| Lycians | 50 | |
| Pamphylians / Dorians of Asia | 30 each | |
| Islanders | 17 | |

**Total warships.** Hdt. **1,207** triremes—same iconic number as Aeschylus *Persians*—likely traditional/literary. Modern estimates for 480 vary widely downward; treat 1,207 as **tradition**, not census.

**Trireme practice.** Ramming, oar-banks, embarked marines; Persian command structure overlays subject captains. Artemisia of Halicarnassus as notable commander in Greek memory (7.99).

**Salamis (480).** Context: narrow straits negate numbers; Greek cohesion and local knowledge; Persian fleet crowded itself; Ionian loyalty questions; command confusion under royal observation. Outcome: strategic disaster forcing Xerxes’ withdrawal trajectory.

**Game-use potential:** Coastal map faction slots—Phoenician elite ships, Egyptian boarding specialists, Ionian “unreliable ally” mechanic; Salamis as scenario where map width is a debuff to imperial fleet stacks.

## 6. Combined arms vs Greek hoplites — failures and successes

### Why Marathon (490) and Plataea (479) failed (Persian perspective)

**Marathon.** Sparse detail; Greek tradition: Athenian/Plataean hoplites close quickly, wings envelop, centre of Persian line (including Sacae?) pushed to ships. Persian strengths (arrows, numbers, cavalry—**cavalry oddly marginal in the surviving story**) fail to shape the battle. Scholarly debates rage over cavalry absence, landing site, and Herodotus’s reliability—**do not freeze one reconstruction as fact**.

**Plataea.** Mardonius’s force includes Persians, Medes, Bactrians, Indians, Sacae, etc. Cavalry harassment works early. In the decisive infantry clash: **spara wall + archery** vs Spartan spear-wall; once *γέρρα* are thrown down, Persians fight bravely but lose the grind; death of Mardonius collapses cohesion; camp is stormed. Konijnendijk et al.: not simply “Persians were cowardly/unarmoured,” but a **doctrine mismatch**—Persian shield-wall optimized to protect shooters, Greek wall optimized to **move and shove**.

**Thermopylae.** Immortals fail frontally in the pass; succeed only with path + envelopment—shows elite status ≠ phalanx-in-a-bottle superiority.

### Where Persians succeeded
- Building and holding a **land empire** from Balkans approaches to Indus with this system for two centuries.
- Combined arms in **Asia**: cavalry + archers + subject heavy troops (e.g. Lydians in near-Greek kit, Hdt. 7.74) + logistics.
- **Mykale** is a Greek victory, but the campaign system that projected power across the Aegean was real.
- Late period: Greek mercenary hoplites **integrated** into Persian battle lines (Cunaxa; Issus)—acknowledgment that native doctrine needed a spear-phalanx module.
- Open-field imperial battles vs non-hoplite enemies; siege and policing; satrapal warfare.

**Doctrine summary.** Persian “ideal” battle: soften with arrows → threaten flanks with horse → break or envelop. Greek hoplite “ideal”: survive missiles → win decisive clash of spears. When Greeks dictated a narrow or head-on spear fight, Persian doctrine underperformed; when space, time, and cavalry dominated, Persian methods excelled.

**Game-use potential:** Rock–paper–scissors baked into history—arrow-wall strong vs light rushes, weak to armoured rush if wall breaks; cavalry strong on open lanes; “Greek mercenary” module as expensive counter-meta unlock.

## 7. Command structure: king, chiliarch, satraps, ethnic levy

### King
Supreme commander; present on major campaigns (Xerxes’ review at Doriscus, 7.59–100). Royal spear-bearer / bow-bearer as court military dignities (Iranica “Courts and Courtiers”).

### Decimal command (Greek report)
Hdt. 7.81: commanders of 10,000 / 1,000 / 100 / 10. Xen. *Cyr.* 2.1.23 adds 50 and 5. Elamite tablets may reflect *daθa-* / *θata-* level units (Schmitt). Reconstructed OPers.: *dathabam* (10), *satabam* (100), *hazarabam* (1,000), *baivarabam* (10,000)—**latter terms partly conjectural**.

### Chiliarch (*χιλίαρχος* ≈ OPers. *hazārapatiš*)
Commander of a thousand; in court context, chief of the **1,000-strong** inner infantry guard and palace security; Greek authors sometimes call him second after the king (Nepos *Conon* 3; Diod. 18.48). Older scholarship over-extended him into “grand vizier” / finance—**Gignoux / Szemerényi caution**. Tied to apple-bearers in Charles’s reconstruction.

### Satrapal contingents
Satraps (*xšaçapāvan-*) raise and lead provincial forces; Xerxes’ catalogue shows **ethnic units under Persian/Achaemenid nobles** as national commanders. Local *ethnē* keep native kit (Assyrian clubs, Libyan charred javelins, etc.).

### Ethnic levy system
Empire as **force multiplier through diversity**: each people contributes characteristic arms. Strategic upside: specialized tools and huge manpower. Downside: uneven training, language barriers, variable loyalty (Ionian ships), logistical heterogeneity.

**Game-use potential:** Command skill tree—King (global aura), Chiliarch (guard buff / court intrigue risk), Satrap (regional unit discounts), Levy diversity (wide roster, cohesion penalties if overmixed).

## 8. Logistics: Royal Road, supply, camp

### Royal Road (*ὁδὸς βασιλείη*)
Hdt. 5.52–53: Sardis–Susa measured in **111 stages**, ~2,500 km, ~**90 days** foot travel; inhabited, guarded, with royal stations (*stathmoi basilikoi*). Likely reuses older Assyrian corridors in part (Livius synthesis). Persepolis tablets: express horse service *pirradaziš*.

### Courier system
Hdt. 8.98: relay horses/riders—Persian *angareion* (Gk. rendering); neither snow nor rain… (the famous praise). Messages Sardis↔Susa in ~**7–9 days** by relay vs ~90 days ordinary travel (standard modern paraphrase of Hdt.’s implications).

### Army supply
- Pre-positioning and local requisition along invasion routes (Xerxes’ Greek campaign: magazines, bridges, canal at Athos—massive engineering logistics).
- Elite separate trains (Hdt. 7.83).
- Fortification tablets / “King’s Table” research (Henkelman; Briant): court mobility as logistical template for royal armies—stations with storage, water, flat camping ground (PersianTRAIL GIS work, 2025).
- Xenophon *Anabasis*: living off land, markets negotiated with satraps—late practical view.

### Camp layout
No Achaemenid Vegetius manual survives. Greek accounts of Plataea: Persian fortified camp (stockade/wagons) as refuge after infantry defeat—enough to repel initial Spartan assaults until stormed. Royal camps implied spacious for court pavilion culture. **Do not invent grid layouts** beyond “fortified laager + royal enclosure + ethnic bivouacs.”

**Game-use potential:** Road stations as buildable supply beacons; relay couriers as vision/map ability; overlong supply lines as Xerxes-tier vulnerability; fortified camp as “second tower” when field army breaks.

## 9. Elite / special: royal bodyguards, apple-bearers, sickle-bearers

### Apple-bearers (*μηλοφόροι* / *mēlophoroi*)
**What.** Guard infantry whose spear-butts were **apple-shaped** counterweights (gold for the innermost thousand in Hdt. 7.41’s pomegranate/apple language—Greek *ῥοιά* pomegranate vs later “apple” label). Alexander historians present *μηλοφόροι* as the king’s infantry guard; Athenaeus/Heracleides preserve court detail.

**Relation to Immortals.** Options: (a) the golden-butt 1,000 are the elite company *within* the 10,000; (b) Immortals ≈ palatine army, apple-bearers ≈ true bodyguard (Charles 2011); (c) successive Greek names for evolving institutions. **No consensus.**

### Kinsmen cavalry (*συγγενεῖς ἱππεῖς*)
Companion/kin cavalry guard paired with apple-bearers in late sources—royal horsemen of trust.

### Chiliarch
See §7—often treated as commander of the thousand guards.

### “Sickle-bearers”
**No standard Achaemenid corps** by this name in Herodotus. Possible confusions:
- Scythed **chariots** (blades, not a guard unit).
- Sacae *sagaris* **axes**.
- Assyrian **iron-studded clubs** (7.63).
- Occasional Greek words for curved blades / *kopides* on subject troops (e.g. Carians, Lycians).

If a game needs “sickle-guard,” flag it as **speculative / fantasy-facing** unless tied to scythe-chariot crews or a specific subject contingent.

### Spear-bearers / bow-bearers of the king
Court titles (*arštibāra*, *vačabāra* reconstructions)—personal arms-bearers, prestige posts (Iranica).

**Game-use potential:** Apple-bearers as king-attached invulnerable-until aura unit; kinsmen cavalry as bodyguard retaliators; avoid inventing sickle-bearers without a tooltip caveat.

## 11. Cross-cutting controversies checklist (read before locking design “facts”)

1. **Army sizes** in Herodotus and Alexander historians are rhetorical.
2. ***Sparabara / takabara / amṛtaka / baivarabam*** are mostly **modern or conjectural** labels—fine for game UI if tooltips say “reconstructed term.”
3. **Immortals’ Iranian name** unsolved; Immortals ≠ automatically every Susa brick archer.
4. ***Kardakes*** ≠ proven continuous replacement of Immortals.
5. ***Cyropaedia*** ≠ straight history for Thymbra chariots/camels.
6. **Ctesias** loses to Herodotus/Xenophon unless corroborated.
7. Greek explanations of Persian defeat (softness, monarchy, luxury) are **ideological**; equipment/doctrine/terrain analyses are stronger for design simulation.
8. Aeschylus’s Salamis is art, not ORBAT.
